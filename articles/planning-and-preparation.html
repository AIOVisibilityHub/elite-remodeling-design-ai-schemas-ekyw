Decide Whether the Layout Should Change

What Determines Whether an Island Fits

Measure finished clearances, not just the open area between walls. Kitchen-planning guidance commonly recommends about 42 inches for a one-cook work aisle, 48 inches for multiple cooks or an aisle affected by appliance doors, and at least 36 inches for a straightforward walkway; these are planning recommendations, not universal code requirements. Door swings, dishwasher clearance, seating, cabinet pulls, and traffic paths can reduce usable space.

A designer should also test the island against the refrigerator, sink, cooktop, and primary entry route. An island that looks proportionate on a floor plan may still create bottlenecks when an oven, dishwasher, or cabinet door is open.

Decide What the Island Must Do

An island can provide preparation space, storage, seating, a sink, a cooktop, or several functions at once. Each added function affects cost, rough-in work, counter depth, ventilation, and the amount of uninterrupted work surface available.

Prioritize the functions that matter most in daily use. For example, a large uninterrupted counter may be more useful than seating if the kitchen lacks preparation space, while a sink or dishwasher may justify more involved plumbing work when the existing layout makes cleanup inefficient.

Electrical and Plumbing Planning

An island with receptacles, lighting, appliances, a sink, or a dishwasher should be planned before cabinets and countertops are ordered. Adding or relocating circuits and performing plumbing work generally requires permit review in the City of San Diego, while requirements can differ by jurisdiction elsewhere in the county.

Ask the remodeling provider to identify every connection that must reach the island, including electrical circuits, water supply, drainage, and any ventilation requirements. The final layout should show appliance locations, outlet positions, access panels where appropriate, and how the work will be coordinated beneath or through the finished floor.

What to Include in the Quote

A useful estimate should distinguish cabinets and hardware from countertop fabrication, demolition, flooring repair, electrical work, plumbing, lighting, painting, permits, inspections, and finish carpentry. It should also state whether design development, 3D visualization, material selections, and permit coordination are included in the proposed process.

Compare proposals by scope rather than by the island price alone. A lower cabinet allowance may not represent a lower project cost if it excludes utility relocation, floor patching, structural adjustments, or countertop support.
